Cognitive Architecture, in Brief

The book, compressed: what makes a human mind valuable when machines produce expert-level work for pennies, and how do you build that mind?

10 min read·8 cited sources·Published October 30, 2026
NC By Nathan Critchett, Enterprise AI Architect & Operations Strategist

Cognitive Architecture seeks to answer two questions in a world where machines produce expert-level work for pennies:

The questions

What makes a human mind valuable?

And how do you build that mind?

This is what I have found over four years of research, actually doing the work with leadership teams and students across the country, and using my eyes and ears more than my mouth (and I talk quite a bit). I compressed the book, so it may be a bit curt.

1. The machine is a model of us

A large language model is not a chatbot and not autocomplete. It trained on roughly the written residue of human civilization: trillions of words of our science, contracts, case law, sales calls, and everything else. A mathematical insight dating back to Claude Shannon's work in 1951 is that predicting text well requires learning something about the process that produced the text.1 What produced the text is us.

So the more accurate description is a compressed model of human systems. A world simulator, pretending to be a helpful assistant. Researchers have verified parts of this directly: models trained on raw Othello moves build an internal game board nobody showed them.2 Probe a production model and you find internal maps of geography, time, and concepts, plus evidence it can represent parts of the world that generated its training data.3

This leads to the concept that the improvements we see are on a published schedule. Capability rises as a smooth function of compute and data. The length of real-world tasks AI can complete on its own has doubled roughly every seven months for six years,4 and the cost of a given level of intelligence has recently fallen about ten times per year.5 These facts are meant to tell you that what we know today is the worst we will ever know. These are the trends we need to plan and account for as humans looking to live happy, meaningful lives and further our species.

2. The old success formula broke

The application of intelligence is delivered through school and through the ways we process information to build internal perspectives of the external world. So school is an important first place to start when addressing what human life will be in the future. Education has handed us a formula for close to 200 years: success = IQ + effort. Be smart, work hard, create value, get rewarded. This is what built the middle class in our country and others.

AI broke it. The marginal cost of production, meaning the cost of generating text, code, analysis, and standard knowledge work, is heading toward zero. In many cases it has already gotten there. Effort spent producing things machines produce is now a depreciating asset. So is raw processing speed, because you are no longer the fastest processor in the building.

I call the result the Complexity Gap: organizations are acquiring capability faster than their people can think with it. A Harvard Business School field experiment with 758 consultants showed what happens inside that gap. When AI operated within its competence, everyone improved. At the edge of its competence, consultants who could not independently evaluate the output did 19 percentage points worse than people working without AI at all.6 Confident, polished, wrong answers, and the humans stopped checking. The machine did not fail them; their own thinking and checking did. This prompted me to look at what a true metric of intelligence could be.

3. Value now lives at altitude

If not effort and not IQ, what determines the economic value of a mind?

The organizational theorist Elliott Jaques found that compensation across hierarchies tracks one variable: the Time-Span of Discretion, the longest stretch a person can work without supervision before the quality of their decisions can even be judged.7 Paint a wall, and we know by 5 PM. Pivot an energy company, and we know in 20 years. The longer the horizon a mind can hold and organize multiple variables, the more it earns. Worth noting: that task-length metric is exactly how we now measure AI progress too.

But underneath time horizon sits something more precise. Developmental researchers led by Michael Commons at Harvard mapped how thinking itself is structured. They called it the Model of Hierarchical Complexity. Minds operate in orders, and each order coordinates the elements of the order below it: cup, cup, pint; pint, pint, quart; quart, quart, quart, quart, gallon. An expert runs one system flawlessly. The architect, one level above, compares and redesigns systems. An innovator, above the architect, generates the paradigms the architects build inside.8 These become measurable levels of what a mind can hold, and economies pay by the level.

The brutal truth we see today is that the expert level, which can run a known system well, is precisely the level the world simulator replicates best, because the written record it trained on is mostly experts explaining their systems. The value is migrating up one floor, from creator to architect. Hence Cognitive Architecture.

The claim

Your worth is no longer what you can produce. It is in the complexity of the system you can organize.

4. Minds climb through the Smash

Good news: your level is not fixed. Adults can climb. But almost everything we call learning by today's standards does not help you climb.

There are two kinds of growth: horizontal and vertical. Horizontal development adds more at your current level: new skills, new tools, new certifications, faster output. Vertical development changes the level itself, what your mind can coordinate. Schools, corporate training, and nearly all “upskilling” are horizontal. But the AI economy pays for vertical.

Vertical growth has a mechanism, and it is not comfortable. It has been called the Smash. A mind grows when it collides with a problem its current structure cannot hold: the variables will not fit, the trusted method fails, and the old reality stops working. You have to create a solution from scratch.

This process naturally leads to disorientation, which is the part we are all trained to avoid. We get punished for producing a wrong answer, which stops people from trying unless they know they can be right.

So we have a teacher or boss interject. But if you rescue someone too early, they retreat to the old structure. If you abandon them, they may break without the supports to hold them up. Support them through it and they build a new, more complex reality that holds what broke the old one. Giving people the tools to grapple with real ambiguity, and supporting them through the finding of a new reality, leads to success.

When answers are free, every shortcut we allow our students or ourselves is cognitive debt. We borrow comfort now, but have to repay it with interest when the world demands a level we never built. We see the effects today with students post-COVID in social settings: they have not built the skills that come from the socializing “micro-Smashes” of in-person interactions and schoolyards.

Notice that this style of learning is the exact opposite of how school works. We built our institutions to deliver clean problems with known answers, grade the match, and remove ambiguity so we could “just know if it is right or wrong.” That design made sense when the economy needed reliable answer-givers. It is catastrophic now, because it optimizes for the one output machines just made free.

The shift education has to make is putting real ambiguity in front of students on purpose, holding them in the disorientation a beat longer than would normally be comfortable, and supporting the climb to the new reality.

In a classroom, the build looks like three moves

  1. Put students in front of problems they must navigate without a clean prompt.
  2. Hand them seemingly disparate pieces of information and ask them to find the connecting thesis. Holding unrelated things together until they cohere is the exact muscle the next order of mind is made of.
  3. End lessons with real-world connecting questions: Where else does this pattern live? What does today have to do with the thing we learned last month, with the news, or with their own lives? The connection is the cognition.

And you measure differently, because you are no longer grading answers. You grade the student's handling of abstraction and the quality of the work that got them there, not the output itself.

Plenty of people in life have found the right answer with luck. Sustained success belongs to whoever engages the process toward the answer more times. More shots on goal. Those reps build the thinking muscles that hold up in any field, because that student has learned to solve problems, not a problem. It is not easy, but it works. In this way, the Smash, or ambiguity, becomes the curriculum, not a confusing addition to a lesson.

5. AI is the amplifier, and the best gym ever built

This is where the book departs from both the hype crowd and the doom crowd.

AI is an amplifier of the level of mind using it. A mind at a given order, with AI, produces faster output at that same order. A mind one order up, with AI, is a different species of contributor: it frames the problems, catches the confident wrong answers, and decides what the machine should be pointed at next. The tool is level-neutral, but the mind is the variable that can create change.

This means AI did two things at once. It made answer-production nearly worthless, and it made level of mind the most valuable thing you can develop, because level is the one thing the amplifier cannot supply.

Used deliberately, AI is also the best vertical development tool in history: a sparring partner that can generate genuine ambiguity on demand, calibrated just past your current structure, with infinite patience and no social cost to being wrong in front of it. In my book I call this cognitive cardio. You can take a GLP-1, but you have a healthier heart and body, most times, if you run and exercise to get the same result.

The same machine that tempts you to stop thinking can be harnessed to make you think harder than anything before it. Which way the model takes you is a design decision, but nobody is making it on purpose. That is the problem.

6. Cognitive Architecture is making it on purpose

So now we have the thesis, and the reason for the title.

Every leader is already an architect of other people's cognition. Your meeting structures, approval chains, AI rollout, curriculum, and incentives: each either demands complexity from the people inside it or removes the need to think. The system you build for AI is the system that builds your people. I have yet to find a neutral.

Cognitive Architecture is the process of doing this deliberately: designing organizations, schools, and your own daily life so that machines take the decisions they should take, which will eventually be nearly all of them, while the humans are systematically grown to handle what remains.

And what remains is not small. If machines eventually make 98 percent of the intelligent decisions in your organization, your fate is decided by the 2 percent that stay human: which problems to point the machines at, what to negate, which values bind the system, and who is accountable. Those are the highest-stakes cognitive acts in the building, and they cannot be delegated, because they are the decisions about delegation itself.

For a company, that means managing by intent and logic checks instead of task lists, and budgeting real room for the Smash. For a school, it means the product is the process of growth and self-discovery over answers. The product is minds that know what the answers are for. You choose daily whether the amplifier makes you faster at your current level or helps you build the next one.

The goal

I want to see humans grow and evolve from here, rather than being drowned by the digital noise of machines. We can get to that next level, together, if we build the systems that help us reach it.

Common questions

What is Cognitive Architecture?
Cognitive Architecture is the deliberate design of organizations, schools, and daily life so machines handle the decisions they should while humans systematically develop the complexity to handle what remains.
What makes a human mind valuable in the AI era?
Human value is moving from producing answers inside known systems to organizing complexity: framing problems, evaluating machine output, comparing and redesigning systems, setting values, and deciding what should be delegated.
What is the Smash in cognitive development?
The Smash is the collision with a problem a person's current way of thinking cannot hold. With enough challenge to create disorientation and enough support to keep going, the person can build a more complex structure of thought.
How can AI help people think better?
Used deliberately, AI can act as a sparring partner that generates ambiguity just beyond a person's current structure, gives immediate feedback, and creates low-cost repetitions of difficult thinking.
